I speak atleast a handful of candidates every week who say they are preparing to move into a product company, but when I ask them which companies they have in mind, the answer is usually a list of familiar names.

There is very little thought about what those companies build, the kind of problems they solve, or what the role would actually demand from them.

Wanting to work for a good company is a good aspiration, but it is not the entire preparation.

The candidates who stand out are usually the ones who have spent time understanding:

1// What their core abilities are. Know yourself and be self aware.

2// What these companies do

3// Building relevant skills in this/these direction.

4// Working on problems that are close to the work these companies want you to do.

An interview gives you an opportunity to prove yourself, but the preparation for that opportunity often begins much much earlier.

A better company may not just require a better interview performance.
It needs you to become a stronger professional before you even apply.

My brother @Sankar2704 started his career in iGate at a measly 2.4L, perhaps today it is 3.5L entry level.

The ₹2.4 lakh salary offered to him then was not enough to live comfortably in a metro. But I cannot forget what that first job made possible for our family and my parents.

TCS, Wipro, Infosys, Cognizant and such companies have opened doors for thousands of young Indians from smaller towns and lesser-known colleges.

For many like my brother, that first job meant moving to a metro, earning their own money, supporting families and getting access to opportunities they might never have had otherwise.

Does that mean freshers should accept low salaries without question?
No.

Companies must recognise the cost of living today.
And I would love to see every IT major pay freshers fairly for the skills they bring to the table.

But here is the other side to this same conversation.

1// Your first salary is a starting point.
It is by no means a measure of your worth or your future potential.

2// If you have no better offer in hand, don't dismiss an opportunity just because the starting salary isn't impressive.

Take it, learn, build your skills, understand how the industry works, and keep growing.

Your next opportunity will be very different from your first.

3// Career growth is not auto pilot.
Your effort, knocking the right opportunities, being relentlessly consistent, continuous learning and some divine blessing. All these matter.

As a recruiter, I have seen careers evolve in ways that no one could have predicted at the beginning.

So yes, question low salaries.
Ask companies to do better. Negotiate whenever you can.

But if this is the door that opens for you today, don't assume it is the only room you will ever enter.

Start where you can.
Build what you can.

And never mistake your starting salary for your earning potential.

Long day.
5 of my Candidates did not clear L2 rounds of interview today.
Cloud Architect role.

2 of them knew they missed the boat and came back back up to ask me this: Where am I actually falling down in interviews?

Honestly, it’s rarely the complex design or architecture.
Or the obscure edge case.

My observation watching all these candidates is that the answer is usually much simpler.

1) Candidates jump into the solution before understanding the problem.
2) They don’t ask enough questions.
3) Basic fundamentals disappear when the pressure builds up.

Very strong engineers faltered when pressure mounted in an interview. .

They heard the question and immediately start building/answering.

Maybe that’s the real interview challenge.

Not knowing everything.
But being able to slow yourself down enough to think clearly.

Pause. Clarify.
Talk through your approach.

Because an interviewer is not only looking at what you build. But they’re also observing how you think when you don’t have the answer immediately.

And sometimes, this small difference is what separates a “maybe” from an offer.

I have spent years as a recruiter.

Today, I also help people rewrite resumes and navigate the hiring process from the candidate side.

Between those two worlds, I’ve reviewed thousands of resumes.
And while there’s plenty of advice on what not to do, here’s what consistently made me move a resume into the “yes” pile within seconds.

1. It answered my first question immediately.

I knew what the candidate did and where they fit before I started digging through their experience.

2. The most recent role carried the story.

The strongest evidence was right at the top, not buried three pages down.

3. It was written for the reader.

Candidates highlighted what the employer needed to see, not just what they were most proud of.

4. The career path made sense.

Even unconventional moves had a logical narrative. I didn’t have to connect the dots myself.

5. Achievements felt real.

Not “improved processes” or “supported initiatives.”

Specific outcomes. Specific impact.

6. It was easy to read.

Simple language. Clean formatting. No buzzword overload.

7. It raised no unnecessary questions.

No unexplained gaps. No confusing titles. No guessing.

Just clarity.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth:

Most resumes don’t get rejected because the candidate lacks ability.

They get rejected because the candidate’s value isn’t obvious enough, quickly enough.

A resume’s job isn’t to tell your entire story.

Its job is to make someone want to hear more.

Before your next application, ask yourself:

If a recruiter spent 10 seconds on my resume, would they immediately understand why I’m worth a conversation?

That’s the test that matters.

The Candidate Was Perfect on Paper. Then the Hiring Manager Asked One Simple Question.

Eight years of experience. Clean CV. Confident handshake.

Then the hiring manager smiled and asked, "So, what do you know about us?"

Pause.
Longer pause.

"You're a, um, SaaS company?"

They were a fintech firm that had just acquired a competitor and was all over the news for three weeks. The CEO of the company had given two interviews about it.

The candidate had not Googled once.

I prep every candidate I send.
Five things minimum.
- Website.
- Social media.
- Recent news.
- Glassdoor.
- One current or former employee on LinkedIn if you can find them.

Not because interviewers expect a PowerPoint presentation on their company history.

But because walking in without research tells the room something you didn't mean to say.

That this job, specifically, doesn't matter enough to you.

The interview starts before you enter the building.

It starts the night before, with a tab open and twenty quiet minutes.

That's it. That's the whole secret.
