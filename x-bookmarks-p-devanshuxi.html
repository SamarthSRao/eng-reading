    I got several DMs asking me to explain what I meant by DFS in my post.

    DFS stands forDepth-First Searchwhich is a graph/tree traversal algorithm.But I also think of it as a mental model for learning.

    The idea is that you pick a path and follow it to the end.

    You don't have to stop every other hour to think, "Oh right, I need to learn 10 prerequisites before I can even understand this concept".You just follow along till you understand it or get stuck because of a critical missing understanding.

    Then you backtrack, filling in the gaps till you can get unstuck, and proceed deeper into the rabbit hole.

    There's another reason why I like this analogy though.

    If you approach learning fundamentals on their own, without having first struggled with the harder concepts that those fundamentals are prerequisites for, you can feel underwhelmed.

    You might feel like, "Oh, this is just a fundamental".

    You see a data structure or an algorithm or a piece of OS theory or a DB primitive and think, "Well there's a neat little toy example where it comes up".

    But if you had first grappled with the harder concept that required that fundamental, your view of the fundamental would be quite different.

    You would see it in a larger context.

    The fundamental wouldn't be a standalone object of appreciation, but rather a building block to something you were trying to understand.

    You don't learn B+ trees because it's on your roadmap of things to learn about databases, but because you wanted to understand storage engines, and you need to know B+ trees to understand storage engines.

    You don't learn WAL as just another log, but as a critical enabler of durability, recovery, and consistency in the face of failures, crashes, and aborts - and how that feeds into the larger system that you're trying to understand.

    The harder concept is providing you with the context to understand the importance of a fundamental.

    And since you've already struggled with the harder concept, you now want to go deeper into understanding the foundational piece.

    You can look at the primitive in less of a vacuum, and more in terms of how it fits into a larger system, how it's used, what limitations it has, the tradeoffs it makes, etc.

    That, to me, is the really interesting part about this type of learning.

    You get to do that kind of analysis only when you first struggled with the harder concept, and then went back and filled in the gaps.

    Curiosity drives us to go deeper.

    Your DFS traversal kicks in.

    Go deep, get stuck, backtrack, repeat.

    DFS your way through learning.

    Wouldn't you say it's more of a dismissal of the importance of code in general rather than a statement about the relative difficulty of coding as work?

    "Code was never the hard part."is something I hear way too oftenand I find those claims ignorant at best.

    Because some of the smartest engineers I've met have always been focused on code,

    specifically in the quality of their implementation:they care about what data structure you picked,unneeded allocations,if this should be an if/else, or an early return, or a different abstraction, what names we choose,invariants,
    memory layout, cache locality, complexity, concurrency, error cases, guards, or the API surface, or whether someone six months from now will have any idea what this code does.

    They care about patterns, but know when to break them.

    They optimize large bodies of code that previously had some clever lines in them, just to make it so that 100 smart lines can be replaced with 30 straightforward ones.

    Or they spend the time to make an O(n^2) path, or an allocation in a tight loop, disappear, or to change the data structure because the working set size is known and needs to be optimized for.

    This is engineering.

    Of course there's some value in learning to understand the problem domain,

    in learning architecture,

    in learning product,

    in learning communication.

    But somehow along the way we've decided that because of these things, code doesn't matter as much anymore.

    It really does.

    The people who write the best code I know are often the ones who pay the most attention to detail, who care the most about their work, and who take the most pride in saying that when they write code, it's good code.

    Code that is simple, clean, fast, has good abstractions that are needed and well motivated, has invariants that are easy to spot, that feels right.

    That's the kind of code where I can just stare at it and say "wow". Where I can delete a bunch of the implementation and somehow make it even better.

    That's serotonin.

    Now that we can make code with AI we can generate thousands of lines of it.

    But I think the ability to know what good code looks like will become exponentially more important, because generating code is not engineering.

    So when I hear someone tell young engineers "don't focus on code, it's never been the hard part"

    I feel like they are teaching completely the wrong lesson.

    Focus on the problem, yes, and on the architecture, but also on your craft.

    Learn your data structures, your langugae, memory, concurrency, profiles, debugging, how to spot unneccessary complexity, how to write code others will enjoy reading, code that is simple, that is fast, that has good abstractions, good invariants, and that generally works.

    Code matters.

    And I think people that are truly great at this tend to reflect that.

    People have a lot of misconceptions about binary search. Most think it only works on sorted arrays, but that’s actually just one special case. The real thing binary search needs is a monotonic search space and figuring out where it could be. A sorted array naturally creates that because conditions transition cleanly like "F F F T T T", meaning once something becomes true, it stays true. That transition point is what binary search is really finding. And this idea goes way beyond arrays like you can binary search over answers, time, capacities, distances, floating point ranges, optimization constraints, or basically anything where a predicate changes monotonically.
    Problems like minimum eating speed, allocate books, aggressive cows, scheduling, load balancing, and many optimization problems all work because the search space itself is ordered, not because there’s a sorted array sitting there.
    Once you understand this, binary search stops being “that O(log n) array algorithm” and becomes a way of thinking about problems:
    engineer monotonicity, then exploit it.

    Just wrapped up the interview process of stealth infra startup (NYC based)

    Team had people from OpenAI and Anthropic and Hedge funds.
    5 rounds in total. Use of LLMs were completely restricted in the first four rounds.

    Round 1 started with a grid DP problem.

    Basic state transition was straightforward, but the brute force complexity was too high for larger constraints.
    Interviewer kept pushing on optimization.

    After thinking for a bit, I noticed the feasibility condition was monotonic:
    if a solution worked for some value "k", it would also work for larger values.

    So instead of directly searching the answer space linearly, I wrapped the DP inside a binary search over the answer.
    That dropped the complexity pretty hard and the interviewer seemed happy with the direction.

    We also spent some time discussing my search engine project:
    indexing pipeline, retrieval flow, ranking heuristics, and some infra decisions behind it.

    Overall probably my strongest round in the loop.

    Round 2 humbled me a bit.

    We have a tree with letters on edges. Count paths where the letters along the path can be rearranged into a palindrome.

    Main observation there was:
    a path is valid if at most one character has odd frequency.

    So instead of storing exact frequencies, I represented parity using bitmasks.
    Each bit represented whether a character count was odd/even along the path.

    I initially went with small-to-large merging on subtrees to optimize the brute force approach.
    But halfway through the discussion I realized there was probably a cleaner way using prefix xor masks + frequency counting on the tree itself.

    Could’ve likely reduced the implementation complexity a lot by treating it more like:
    “how many previous masks differ by at most one bit.”

    One of those rounds where you know the core idea, but afterwards your brain keeps replaying all the cleaner approaches you could’ve explored.

    Round 3 was systems design:
    I had to design a database schema branching system.

    Basically:

    Spent today going way too deep into Firecracker internals and honestly… the whole “it’s secure because it’s small” narrative starts feeling a bit shaky once you actually trace how this thing runs under the hood. like yeah, on paper it’s clean as hell we got tiny rust VMM, no qemu baggage, one microVM per process, minimal device model, everything looks tight and intentional. but then you follow the execution flow across threads and realize a lot of the security story quietly depends on timing behaving nicely… which is not something you should ever rely on in systems like this.

    The core architecture is simple enough: you’ve got an API thread acting as a single-threaded HTTP control plane, a VMM thread that owns all the interesting stuff like virtio devices and the MMDS metadata service, and then a bunch of vCPU threads that sit in a tight KVM_RUN loop bouncing between guest and host. everything funnels through KVM, but the real surface isn’t the hypervisor boundary it’s the coordination between these threads before the system actually settles into a steady state. that “in-between” phase is where things get weird.

    What surprised me is that Firecracker’s isolation model isn’t atomic, it’s staged. first the jailer sets things up like cgroups, chroot, capability drops, then the VMM initializes, then seccomp filters are applied, and only after that do you really reach something you’d call a locked-down system. the problem is these steps overlap. they’re not clean cut transitions. there are small but very real windows where the process is partially initialized, partially restricted, and still processing input.

    take the jailer, for example. it’s supposed to be this clean containment boundary, but if you look closely at the order of operations, it’s doing bind mounts and filesystem setup before privileges are fully dropped. that means there’s a window where the filesystem view can still be influenced. in a shared environment like a noisy k8s node or anything multi-tenant . That’s enough to make symlink race attacks viable. so the “chroot = isolation” story kind of turns into “chroot = isolation unless someone can interfere at the right moment,” which is a much weaker guarantee than it sounds.

    then there’s the VMM itself, which starts handling things like MMDS (the metadata service exposed over vsock) before seccomp is fully enforced. that’s where things go from “hmm that’s messy” to “okay this is actually interesting.” MMDS is supposed to be a boring, read-only key-value store that the guest can query, but there’s a race window where you can interact with it before the syscall surface is locked down. if you hit that window, the VMM will happily accept PUT requests without strictly validating them against the original config. so now you can inject arbitrary entries into what later becomes “trusted metadata” from the guest’s point of view.

    and that creates a pretty weird primitive. it’s not a clean escape or anything dramatic, but it’s a controlled reflection channel inside a system that assumes metadata is static and safe. once the VM is fully booted, you can just curl the metadata endpoint and get back whatever you injected earlier, as if it was legitimate config. that opens the door to environment probing, subtle data exfil patterns, or feeding controlled inputs into higher-level systems that trust MMDS more than they probably should.

    the deeper issue here isn’t just MMDS or the jailer or any single bug , It’s that Firecracker’s design assumes the system becomes secure at some point, instead of being secure from the first instruction that can be influenced by an attacker. there’s this implicit trust that threads wake up in the right order, that signals are delivered cleanly, that seccomp lands before anything meaningful happens. but under load, or with intentional timing pressure, those assumptions get shaky fast. and once timing becomes part of your attack surface, things get subtle in a way that’s much harder to reason about than classic memory corruption bugs.

    what makes this more relevant now is how Firecracker is actually being used. it’s not just lambdas anymore, people are running GPU-backed workloads, model inference sandboxes, all kinds of ML infra on top of it. so now you’ve got valuable data sitting nearby such as model weights, prompts, execution traces and a system where early-boot behavior, metadata channels, and cross-thread coordination can all be nudged in ways that weren’t fully accounted for. you don’t always need a full VM escape to cause damage in that world; sometimes just learning something about the host or influencing a supposedly “read-only” path is enough.

    so yeah, Firecracker absolutely reduces attack surface compared to something like QEMU, no question. but what’s left is this smaller, sharper set of edges where correctness depends heavily on ordering and timing. it’s memory-safe, which is great, but it’s not state-safe in the way people casually assume. and if you’re looking for real bugs, that’s exactly where I’d keep digging.

    one thing that really changed my perspective is how insanely CPU heavy crawling actually become.

    People usually think crawling is mostly netwrk I/O.
    It’s not.

    The moment pages start coming in at scale, the bottleneck slowly shifts toward CPU + memory efficiency.

    Every single page turns into a pipeline of expensive operations:
    HTML tokenization,
    DOM parsing,
    URL normalization,
    query param sorting,
    canonical extraction,
    robots matching,
    SHA-256 hashing,
    compression/decompression,
    outlink extraction,
    dedupe checks,
    priority scoring,
    SQLite writes,
    retry classification,
    domain scheduling,
    and frontier state transitions.

    Even something as “simple” as parsing HTML becomes brutal at scale.

    A malformed 2MB HTML document with thousands of links means:

    - tons of allocations
    - string processing everywhere
    - cache misses
    - heavy traversal work
    - normalization on every extracted URL
    - hashing + dedupe checks on every discovered outlink

    Now multiply that by millions of pages.

    And then you realize the crawler is basically a distributed systems + systems engineering problem disguised as a networking project.

    The reason I picked @rustlang for this is because performance actually compounds at crawler scale.

    Zero-cost abstractions,
    predictable memory layout,
    tight control over allocations,
    fearless concurrency,
    and not having a garbage collector pausing workers randomly under load makes a massive difference.

    The async runtime can keep thousands of fetches alive while the parser threads are chewing through HTML and frontier scheduling simultaneously without the system falling apart.

    A huge amount of time also went into designing the crawler to avoid unnecessary work:

    - persistent dedupe state
    - lease recovery
    - domain-level politeness scheduling
    - adaptive retries
    - filtering static assets early
    - canonicalization before insertion
    - priority scoring before scheduling

    At this point the project feels less like “I built a crawler” and more like:
    “I accidentally started building a distributed compute engine optimized for parsing the internet.”

    Next step is distributed workers + Kafka/Redpanda pipeline + adaptive rate limiting.

    Rust honestly feels absurdly good for this category of high-performance infrastructure software.
