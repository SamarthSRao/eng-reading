OverOptLab

Reinforcement Learning · Implementation-of-paper · Paper
a reward-overoptimization lab: best-of-n against a gold reward, with ensembles

  • Domain: Reinforcement Learning
  • Kind: Implementation-of-paper
  • Source: Coste et al., "Reward Model Ensembles Help Mitigate Overoptimization" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743) (Paper)
  • Curiosity question: As you sample more candidates and pick the best by a proxy reward, when does the true reward start to fall, and how much does an ensemble delay that?
  • You build: a pipeline with a fixed "gold" scorer, several proxy reward models trained on noisy preferences from it, best-of-n sampling for n from 1 to 1024, and single vs mean vs worst-case vs uncertainty-penalized ensembles, with bootstrap confidence intervals.
  • You learn: Goodhart's law in numbers, KL as a budget (for best-of-n, KL = log n - (n-1)/n), why ensembles help.
  • Baseline: a single proxy reward model, and random sampling.
  • Demo: plot gold vs proxy reward against KL for each method and point to where the single proxy peaks and turns down.
  • Measured by: gold reward at matched KL, the KL at which the proxy-gold gap opens, bootstrap CIs over 20 seeds, wall-clock per run.
  • Verified by: a check that empirical KL matches the closed form for best-of-n, fixed-seed reproducibility, and a sanity run where the proxy equals the gold (the gap must be zero).
  • What would surprise me: if the ensemble helps less than adding more preference data.
  • Scope cuts: no PPO, only best-of-n; small models only.
  • Why it's worth building: reward hacking is the central failure of RLHF; having your own curves is worth more than reading about them.
  • Visual proof: gold vs proxy curves with confidence bands, and a screenshot of the sweep dashboard.
  • Ship: a GitHub release v0.1.0 with a Pages report; the README embeds the chart and a screenshot; 2-3 weeks.

ShedLine

Infrastructure + Go · Library · Blog
an overload controller for Go services: CoDel queue, adaptive LIFO, priority-aware inflight limits

  • Domain: Infrastructure + Go
  • Kind: Library
  • Source: Uber engineering blog, "How Uber Conquered Database Overload: The Journey from Static Rate-Limiting to Intelligent Load Management" (https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/from-static-rate-limiting-to-intelligent-load-management/) (Blog)
  • Curiosity question: Uber reports about 80% more throughput and about 70% lower P99 under overload versus a token bucket. How much of that comes from the CoDel queue, how much from LIFO, and how much from priority-aware limits, on a service you control?
  • You build: a Go middleware with a bounded queue using CoDel-style drop timing, adaptive LIFO when the queue is long, queue timeouts derived from P90 latency, and per-priority inflight limits; plus an open-loop load generator and a toy database-backed service to protect.
  • You learn: queueing under overload, why open-loop load matters, goroutine and heap behaviour when you do not shed.
  • Baseline: a plain token-bucket limiter at the same nominal rate, and no limiter at all.
  • Demo: drive 2x and 5x capacity at an open-loop rate; show goodput, P99 of successful requests, goroutines and heap for each controller, and high-priority latency staying flat.
  • Measured by: goodput (successful RPS), P50/P99/P99.9 of successful requests, goroutine count and heap from pprof, share of high-priority requests served, with hardware and the exact service time stated.
  • Verified by: a deterministic discrete-event simulator of the queue discipline with property tests (no request served twice, total served + shed = arrived, high priority never starved while low is admitted), a comparison against an analytic M/M/1 queue below saturation, and a race-detector run.
  • What would surprise me: if adaptive LIFO helps tail latency but hurts total goodput.
  • Scope cuts: one node, no distributed rate limiting, no config UI.
  • Why it's worth building: every large backend has this layer; it is a standard interview topic and Uber's post gives you numbers to chase.
  • Visual proof: goodput and P99 versus offered load for each controller, and a terminal GIF of the 5x overload run.
  • Ship: a GitHub release v0.1.0 (Go module) with a Pages page; the README embeds the chart and the GIF; 2-3 weeks.

PackedCache

Backend + Napkin Math · Library · Blog
a DNS-style cache in Rust that stores answers in wire format with no padding and measures every byte

  • Domain: Backend + Napkin Math
  • Kind: Library
  • Source: Cloudflare engineering blog, "How we saved 100 terabytes of memory by optimizing 1.1.1.1's DNS cache" (https://blog.cloudflare.com/dns-cache-memory-optimization-1111/) (Blog)
  • Curiosity question: Cloudflare halved the per-entry footprint in five steps. Which of those steps give most of the saving on your hardware, and does the cache really get faster while it shrinks?
  • You build: a TTL cache keyed by (name, type, class) where each entry is one allocation holding the answer in packed wire format, with a naive struct-per-record version beside it, a Zipf-distributed resolver workload generator, and per-step toggles (padding removal, record packing, name compression, single allocation, compact TTL).
  • You learn: struct layout and padding, allocator behaviour at 10 million entries, why locality turns memory savings into speed.
  • Baseline: the naive entry layout with Vec<Record> and owned String names, plus a plain HashMap of the same entries.
  • Demo: run both caches at 10 million entries and show bytes per entry, RSS, insert throughput and lookup p99 side by side, then toggle the five steps one at a time.
  • Measured by: bytes per entry (computed and from RSS), insert and lookup throughput, p50/p99 lookup latency, allocation counts, on a stated CPU, with a workload shaped by a record-type mix you cite.
  • Verified by: a differential test against a reference resolver library's parsed answers for 1 million random answers (byte-identical re-serialization), a property test that TTLs never go up and expire exactly on time under a fake clock, and a fuzzer on the wire parser.
  • What would surprise me: if packing the answer makes lookup slower because you must re-parse on every hit (then caching the compressed name offset is the fix).
  • Scope cuts: no recursive resolution, no DNSSEC, no networking beyond a UDP loopback demo.
  • Why it's worth building: memory is the largest cost of any cache fleet; this is the exact optimization a large resolver team shipped, and "bytes per entry" is a number you can defend in an interview.
  • Visual proof: a stacked bar of bytes per entry per optimization step, and a terminal GIF of the cache benchmark.
  • Ship: a GitHub release v0.1.0 and a Pages site; the README shows the per-step benchmark chart and the GIF; 2-3 weeks.

SinkWindow

SLMs · Implementation-of-paper · Paper
attention-sink KV-cache eviction in a small LLM runtime, checked on long text

  • Domain: SLMs
  • Kind: Implementation-of-paper
  • Source: Xiao et al., "Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453) (Paper)
  • Curiosity question: Why does dropping the first tokens from the KV cache make a model collapse, and why does keeping just four of them fix it? How much memory and speed does that buy on a laptop-class model?
  • You build: a decode loop over a small open model (1B-class) with a KV cache that keeps a few "sink" tokens plus a sliding window, re-indexes positions inside the cache, and runs perplexity on long documents.
  • You learn: how attention concentrates on early tokens, KV memory arithmetic (2 x layers x KV heads x head dim x bytes x tokens), position handling when you evict.
  • Baseline: dense attention with the full KV cache, and window-only eviction without sinks.
  • Demo: stream a 20K-token document: dense runs out of memory, window-only perplexity explodes, sink + window stays flat; print KV bytes and tokens/s.
  • Measured by: perplexity per 1K-token chunk across the stream, KV-cache bytes, decode tokens/s, peak RSS or VRAM, with the model and hardware stated.
  • Verified by: unit tests that your KV-bytes formula equals the measured tensor sizes, a test that the cache with window larger than the text reproduces dense outputs exactly (bitwise logits), and a comparison to the paper's qualitative collapse and recovery.
  • What would surprise me: if the number of sink tokens that fixes the collapse differs by model family.
  • Scope cuts: no training from scratch, no multi-GPU, one model family.
  • Why it's worth building: long-running chat on small devices is limited by KV memory; this is the cheap fix every serving stack asks about.
  • Visual proof: a perplexity-over-position chart for the three strategies, and a GIF of the streaming run.
  • Ship: a GitHub release v0.1.0 with notebooks and a Pages page; the README embeds the chart and the GIF; 2-3 weeks.

LittleCheck

Napkin Math + Backend · Tool · Book
a tool that checks load-test results against queueing laws and exposes coordinated omission

  • Domain: Napkin Math + Backend
  • Kind: Tool
  • Source: Harchol-Balter, Performance Modeling and Design of Computer Systems, ch. 6 "Little's Law and Other Operational Laws" (Book)
  • Curiosity question: If you run a benchmark, do its throughput, latency and concurrency numbers obey N = X x R and the utilization law? When they do not, is the benchmark lying (coordinated omission) or the server?
  • You build: a CLI that ingests k6/wrk/your-own-client logs plus CPU metrics, computes operational-law residuals, an open-loop and closed-loop generator pair, and a discrete-event M/M/c simulator to compare against Erlang C.
  • You learn: operational laws, open vs closed loop, why p99 depends on how you generate load.
  • Baseline: the raw tool report (mean latency, RPS) with no checks.
  • Demo: run the same Go server under closed-loop and open-loop load; LittleCheck flags the closed-loop run as understating p99 and prints the corrected distribution.
  • Measured by: |N - X x R| / N per run, utilization-law residual, p99 under open vs closed loop, simulator vs Erlang C error, on stated hardware.
  • Verified by: a simulator that matches the closed-form Erlang C within 2% across 20 utilization levels, property tests on the law checks with synthetic traces, and injection of a known 5-second pause that the tool must detect.
  • What would surprise me: if a well-known load tool passes the checks only at low utilization.
  • Scope cuts: one server, no distributed tracing, text and JSON input only.
  • Why it's worth building: capacity planning starts with trustworthy benchmarks; this is the sanity check most teams skip.
  • Visual proof: a p99-versus-utilization chart (measured vs simulated), and a screenshot of the report.
  • Ship: a GitHub release v0.1.0 and a Pages page; the README embeds the chart and a screenshot; 2-3 weeks.

EscSimd

Terminals · Library · Book
a terminal escape-sequence parser in Rust with a SIMD fast path, fuzzed against a table-driven reference

  • Domain: Terminals
  • Kind: Library
  • Source: Hennessy & Patterson, Computer Architecture: A Quantitative Approach (6th ed.), ch. 4 "Data-Level Parallelism in Vector, SIMD, and GPU Architectures" (Book)
  • Curiosity question: kitty's author reports about 2x from vector instructions on an escape-code parser. Where do the gains come from: skipping plain text quickly, or classifying bytes in bulk, and on which workloads does it lose?
  • You build: a VT/ANSI state machine after the DEC parser diagram, a scalar reference, and a SIMD version that scans for control bytes 16 or 32 at a time and only runs the state machine on escape runs; a vtebench-like workload generator; a pty harness that records real program output.
  • You learn: SIMD byte classification, branch-free scanning, when state machines beat vectorization.
  • Baseline: the scalar table-driven parser and a memchr-for-ESC hybrid.
  • Demo: MB/s per workload (plain text, dense SGR colors, cursor motion) for each parser on a stated CPU.
  • Measured by: throughput in MB/s per workload, instructions per byte and branch-misses from perf stat, cache misses.
  • Verified by: differential fuzzing of the SIMD parser against the scalar reference on random and mutated byte streams (event streams must be identical), chunk-boundary tests that split every sequence at every offset, and replay of recorded real sessions.
  • What would surprise me: if the hybrid memchr version matches SIMD on typical output.
  • Scope cuts: no rendering, no Unicode width handling, no sixel.
  • Why it's worth building: terminal throughput is dominated by the parser; this is the skill behind fast emulators.
  • Visual proof: a grouped bar chart of MB/s by workload, and a GIF of the pty recording being parsed.
  • Ship: a GitHub release (crate) v0.1.0 with a Pages page; the README embeds the benchmark chart and a GIF; 2-3 weeks.

SkipLin

Java · Library · Book
a lock-free skip-list map in Java, checked with Lincheck and raced against the JDK

  • Domain: Java
  • Kind: Library
  • Source: Herlihy & Shavit, The Art of Multiprocessor Programming (2nd ed.), ch. 14 "Skiplists and Balanced Search" (Book)
  • Curiosity question: The JDK's ConcurrentSkipListMap is a lock-free skip list. How close can your version get, and which design decisions (marking, helping, level choice) move the numbers?
  • You build: a lock-free sorted map with logical-deletion marks, helping on traversal, a randomized level generator, and range iteration; JMH benchmarks with read-heavy, write-heavy and range workloads.
  • You learn: CAS-based design, memory ordering with VarHandle, linearizability testing.
  • Baseline: ConcurrentSkipListMap, a synchronized TreeMap, and a ReentrantReadWriteLock-wrapped TreeMap.
  • Demo: ops/s versus thread count (1 to the core count) for each map on a stated CPU, plus a Lincheck run that finds a seeded bug.
  • Measured by: JMH throughput and p99 latency per operation mix, CAS failure counts, cache misses from perf, hardware stated.
  • Verified by: Lincheck linearizability tests on random operation scenarios, a stress test with a sequential model, and a seeded-bug check (remove the mark step; the test must fail).
  • What would surprise me: if the lock-based TreeMap wins at 2 threads.
  • Scope cuts: no memory reclamation beyond GC, no persistence.
  • Why it's worth building: concurrent data structures are a staple of Java interviews and real code review.
  • Visual proof: a throughput-versus-threads chart, and a GIF of Lincheck failing then passing.
  • Ship: a GitHub release v0.1.0 (jar) with a Pages page; the README embeds the chart and the GIF; 2-3 weeks.

DipperKV

Databases + Backend · Engine/DB · Blog
a flash-aware key-value cache with a log-structured store and a RAM index

  • Domain: Databases + Backend
  • Kind: Engine/DB
  • Source: Meta engineering blog, "McDipper: A key-value cache for Flash storage" (https://engineering.fb.com/2013/03/05/web/mcdipper-a-key-value-cache-for-flash-storage/) (Blog)
  • Curiosity question: Meta cut servers by up to 90% by putting low-rate pools on flash and still answered more than 90% of gets in under a millisecond. What hit ratio, device write amplification and p99 do you get per dollar of flash versus RAM on your own SSD?
  • You build: a memcached-protocol cache that appends items to large aligned segments on an SSD, keeps a compact in-memory hash index, evicts whole segments FIFO, and rebuilds its index from the log after a crash.
  • You learn: SSD write amplification, why large aligned writes matter, crash-safe log recovery, index memory per item.
  • Baseline: a RAM-only cache at the same memory budget, and the same data on a pread-per-item design.
  • Demo: replay a Zipf trace larger than RAM; show hit ratio, p50/p99 get latency and SSD bytes written, then kill -9 mid-run and recover with the index rebuilt.
  • Measured by: hit ratio, p50/p99/p99.9 get latency, device write amplification (bytes written to the SSD over user bytes, from iostat/SMART), index bytes per item, recovery time, with the SSD model and fio baseline stated.
  • Verified by: a differential test against an in-memory model (random get/set/delete traces must agree on every hit), crash-injection at random points in the log (recovered state must be a prefix-consistent subset of acknowledged writes), and checksum validation on every read.
  • What would surprise me: if the index, not the flash, becomes the memory bottleneck so flash capacity cannot be used.
  • Scope cuts: no replication, no TLS, binary protocol subset only.
  • Why it's worth building: flash tiers are how real caches lower cost per GB; the same design appears in several production cache systems.
  • Visual proof: a chart of hit ratio and p99 versus RAM:flash ratio, and a GIF of the crash-and-recover run.
  • Ship: a GitHub release v0.1.0 with a Pages page; the README includes the benchmark chart and the GIF; 2-3 weeks.

Product ideas

  • PhotoShrink — shrink a photo library by about a fifth without changing a single pixel
    Domain: Go · grows from: Dropbox, "Lepton image compression"
    Who it's for: people whose phone or cloud storage is full of JPEGs.
    What you see/do: drag in a folder; it shows "38.1 GB to 29.7 GB, restore verified" and keeps originals recoverable byte for byte.
    Technical core: JPEG Huffman decode to DCT coefficients, context-modelled arithmetic coding (DC predicted from edge pixels, AC by neighbours), exact re-encode.
    Measured by: percent saved on a 1,000-photo set, decode MB/s, 100% SHA-256 round-trip on every file.
    Mentor-style core: Baseline: zip or xz on JPEGs (about 1% saving). Clever part: modelling coefficients instead of bytes. Shows: about 20% saved with bit-exact restore.
    Why people want this: HN: a commenter wants tooling to re-encode an existing JPEG photo library losslessly; r/jpegxl thread on Dropbox's Lepton compressing JPEG losslessly.
    MVP: a CLI plus tiny desktop shell: pack, verify, unpack.
    Ship: a GitHub release with a download page.
  • ShellTape — record a terminal session and share a version where secrets are already blanked
    Domain: Terminals · grows from: kitty issue #7005 (escape-code parser)
    Who it's for: anyone who records a terminal for a tutorial or a support ticket.
    What you see/do: a playable, searchable recording where API keys and passwords are replaced on screen, not just in the raw bytes.
    Technical core: replay the bytes through a VT state machine into a cell grid and redact what is displayed, so secrets split across cursor-movement escapes are caught.
    Measured by: leak rate over 50 recorded sessions with 200 planted secrets (target 0), index time per MB.
    Mentor-style core: Baseline: regex over the raw byte stream. Clever part: redact on the screen grid after the parser. Shows: raw regex misses secrets typed with echo and cursor jumps; grid redaction does not.
    Why people want this: Feature request: Redact specified strings / regexes; Including command in asciinema recordings is a security problem; Don't leak host, username and current working directory.
    MVP: record, redact, play in a browser.
    Ship: a GitHub release and a hosted demo page.
  • SinceLast — a shared list that works offline on two phones and never duplicates an item
    Domain: Distributed Systems · grows from: lbenicio, Paxos TLA+ proof
    Who it's for: couples and housemates sharing a grocery list.
    What you see/do: edits from both phones merge when they reconnect; check-offs never come back.
    Technical core: a CRDT list with unique item IDs and add-wins tombstones, plus a causal sync log.
    Measured by: duplicate-item rate and lost-edit rate over 1,000 randomized offline sessions (target 0 each), sync bytes per edit.
    Mentor-style core: Baseline: last-write-wins by timestamp. Clever part: per-item IDs and causal merge. Shows: LWW loses edits and duplicates under clock skew; the CRDT does not.
    Why people want this: Listonic reviews: "adding items multiple times, up to 20 duplicates; Cartful, an app marketing offline check-offs.
    MVP: two simulated phones plus a web page.
    Ship: a hosted demo.
  • ChatKeep — an on-device chat that does not run out of memory or forget how to talk when it gets long
    Domain: SLMs · grows from: Turing Pi, "LLM tokens per second and memory bandwidth"
    Who it's for: people running a small local model on a phone or a single-board computer.
    What you see/do: a chat that keeps running at a steady tokens/s with a live "memory used" gauge.
    Technical core: a KV-cache budget calculator from the formula, plus attention-sink eviction with position re-indexing.
    Measured by: tokens before out-of-memory on an 8 GB device, perplexity change versus full context, tokens/s over a 20K-token chat.
    Mentor-style core: Baseline: truncate old messages. Clever part: keep sink tokens and a window. Shows: stable quality and speed far past the dense limit.
    Why people want this: Misc. bug: context shift results in error; investigate and mitigate LLM memory pressure / OOM in long sessions.
    MVP: a CLI over one small model.
    Ship: a GitHub release.
  • SeqGate — a deploy gate that decides "roll back or keep going" without false alarms
    Domain: Infrastructure · grows from: Netflix, "Rapid Regression Detection in Software Deployments through Sequential Testing"
    Who it's for: small teams with Prometheus metrics and a rollout tool.
    What you see/do: a live "evidence" meter for each canary; rollbacks fire when the evidence crosses a boundary, with the reason printed.
    Technical core: an always-valid sequential test on canary-versus-baseline error counts, with traffic-imbalance handling.
    Measured by: detection delay and false-positive rate on 100 replayed incident and healthy traces, compared with a fixed 1% threshold.
    Mentor-style core: Baseline: "error rate above 1%" (22 false positives, 65% in the anignoramuss write-up). Clever part: stop only when the likelihood ratio crosses 1/alpha. Shows: fewer false rollbacks at equal detection delay.
    Why people want this: r/kubernetes: "What do you people gate automated rollback on?; Canary Deployment with Automated Rollback.
    MVP: a CLI that reads two Prometheus queries.
    Ship: a GitHub release with a demo page.
  • HotCheck — tells you which Postgres tables are losing to index write amplification and how to fix them
    Domain: PostgreSQL · grows from: CST Solution, "What Each Index Costs"
    Who it's for: engineers with a busy Postgres table and too many indexes.
    What you see/do: per-table HOT-update ratio, the indexes that block HOT, a fillfactor suggestion and the WAL bytes you would save.
    Technical core: reads pg_stat_user_tables, pg_stat_user_indexes and pg_stats; estimates WAL per index (about 76 bytes per row per btree index in that benchmark) and HOT elig…
    Measured by: predicted vs measured WAL saved after applying the advice on a pgbench-style schema, in percent error.
    Mentor-style core: Baseline: n_tup_hot_upd ratio alone. Clever part: tie blocked HOT to the specific index and column. Shows: a concrete "drop this index or change this column" with a measured saving.
    Why people want this: Adyen's write-up of tuning HOT updates; Hexacluster's HOT benchmark.
    MVP: a single SQL script and a CLI report.
    Ship: a GitHub release.
