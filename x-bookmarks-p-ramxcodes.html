    People keep asking me:

    "Why are you learning Go?"
    "Will tRPC get you a job?"
    "Are you even going to use WebSockets at work?"

    Fair questions.

    But they're based on an assumption I don't agree with.

    I don't learn things because they're trending.
    I don't learn them because an employer wants them.
    I learn them because I'm curious.

    That's also why I never forced myself to grind DSA.

    Everyone kept saying:
    "You'll fail OAs."
    "You won't get a job."

    Maybe.

    But I couldn't make myself study something I had zero interest in.

    Ironically, after spending years programming, I actually enjoy solving problems now.
    Not because someone told me to. Because I naturally grew into it.

    One thing I've noticed:

    Whenever I start learning something, a few people start learning it too.

    Go.
    tRPC.
    WebSockets.
    Whatever it is.

    That's fine. If I introduced you to something interesting, I'm happy.

    But don't learn it just because I am.

    You don't see the hundreds of hours of time I'll spend on something that might never make me a single rupee, but I do it anyways because I like it.

    Last year a few people started learning Go after seeing me.

    Most quit within weeks.

    The people who genuinely liked it are still using it today.

    That's the difference.

    Build your stack around your interests, not someone else's roadmap.

    I never became a "MERN developer."

    I liked Express.
    I liked Next.js.
    I liked TanStack.
    So I built around those.

    Frameworks come and go.

    The ability to understand problems and ship software stays.

    Take inspiration from people.

    Don't outsource your curiosity to them.

    50 years ago someone decided everything on your computer is a file.

    Turns out "everything" included the internet.

    Your network?
    Also a file. Yeah......

    𝚕𝚜𝚘𝚏 means 𝚕𝚒𝚜𝚝 𝚘𝚙𝚎𝚗 𝚏𝚒𝚕𝚎𝚜.

    On Unix-like systems, network sockets (like ports) are treated as files

    -𝚒 tells 𝚕𝚜𝚘𝚏 to list network-related files (internet/network connections).

    :𝟹𝟶𝟶𝟶 narrows it to anything using port 3000 (TCP or UDP).

    so `𝚕𝚜𝚘𝚏 -𝚒 :𝟹𝟶𝟶𝟶` will give you PID of the network
    process.

    This is useful when you want to find which Express or Next.js server is running on that port.

    Your port might be 3000, while the process ID could be 26382.

    Because ports belong to processes, you don't kill the port, you kill the process that's holding it.

    But who in the world thought network = file system??

  • 30 Jun 2026
    I think doing tasks tires you far less than thinking about doing them.

    Your brain doesn't really care whether you're lifting weights or spending an hour imagining yourself lifting weights.

    It just knows you've been mentally occupied with that domain.

    You think about going to the gym or coding, writing, reading.

    By the time you actually start, you're already mentally exhausted.

    I think this is why so many people feel tired despite getting very little done.

    The realization changed how I work.

    Now, whenever I catch myself thinking, "I should do this," I stop thinking and start moving.

    > I dump the task into TickTick.
    > Time block it.
    > When the block starts, I execute.

    No more negotiating with myself. No more mental rehearsals.

    I've noticed something interesting.

    I can have a day packed with work, gym, reading, and meetings and still not feel fatigued.

    Busy isn't what drains me. Overthinking does.

  • 28 Nov 2025
    Stop running behind shiny objects

    Instead of quickly jumping from one shiny object to another.

    I started doing one thing:

    I would pick something and dedicate myself to it religiously for at least three to six months.

    If I reached a certain level where I felt I could quit, then I would quit.

    For example, when I started with video editing, I learned Premiere Pro and Adobe After Effects, worked with 18+ clients and then I quit.

    When I learned graphic designing, I mastered Photoshop, Canva, and Figma, build tons of posters & graphics and then I quit.

    The same went for app development.
    After making two or three apps,
    I switched to full-stack web development.

    Now that I am in full-stack web development, I am currently improving the backend portion.

    I will switch to another field only when I reach a certain point where I feel,

    "Okay, I have done enough now I can do anything I wanted to do."

    This is because if you switch early, you will become a collector, not a master of things.

    When I was learning all these things, I used to spend around seven to eight hours on a daily basis.

    With that dedication, it took me approximately four to five days to complete React, three days to complete CSS, and similarly, three days to complete Next.js.

    However, this was not exactly completion it was just the theory portion and the basic code.

    After creating some projects for a month or two, I became pretty confident with the skill.

    So, stop chasing. Start choosing and sticking to it.
